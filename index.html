Decision Guide

Which NIH Fellowship Is Right for You?

Your stage of training, degree type, and citizenship determine your primary target. Multiple applications are not just permitted — they are strategic. You cannot hold two NIH awards simultaneously, but you can apply to several and accept the best offer.

Single PhD, years 2–4, US citizen or PR

F31 — Your primary target

The standard predoctoral fellowship. Apply after qualifying exams when you have preliminary data. Strongest applications come from students with 1–2 years of dissertation results. Plan for a 10–12 month gap between submission and first payment.

➜ F31
Want to do dissertation research at NIH in Bethesda

GPP — Best stipend, open to international

The GPP places you in an NIH Intramural Research Program lab for your dissertation. Stipend ($51K+) substantially exceeds the F31. Individual partnerships open year-round — find a PI first. Open to foreign nationals; the only major NIH mechanism that is.

➜ GPP
MD/PhD, DO/PhD, or other dual-degree program

F30 — Designed for dual-degree students

Same stipend as F31 but with a higher tuition cap ($21K vs $16K) and up to 6 years of support. Apply within 48 months of matriculation. Use PA-25-425 if Meharry does not have an NIH-funded MSTP (check with your program).

➜ F30
Years 3–5 of PhD, ready to plan your postdoc

F99/K00 — Bridges PhD to postdoc

Two-phase award: F99 covers 1–2 final dissertation years, then K00 funds 3–4 years of a postdoc at any institution. You don't need to know where you'll do your postdoc when you apply. Next deadline: December 8, 2026. Not all ICs participate — verify first.

➜ F99/K00
Post-PhD, seeking postdoc or bridge funding

F32 — Standard postdoc fellowship

Applies after your PhD is complete. Same review structure as F31 (3 criteria). Stipend rises with years of experience. Also consider the K99/R00 if you're targeting a faculty position — it funds your postdoc and your first faculty years. Note the 1-year payback requirement.

➜ F32
Your PI has an active NIH grant, you're underrepresented

Diversity Supplement — Fastest path

The quickest route to funded training: your PI submits an administrative supplement to an existing award. No set deadline. You must not already be in the grant budget. Ask your potential advisor about their active NIH awards. If F31-Diversity is currently uncertain, this is the more stable alternative.

➜ Diversity Supp.
Primary Predoctoral Fellowship

F31 — Ruth L. Kirschstein NRSA Predoctoral Fellowship

The most common NIH fellowship for PhD students. Winning an F31 signals to every future employer — faculty search committees, postdoc supervisors, industry research teams — that your dissertation research survived independent peer review before you finished your degree.

What the F31 actually does for your career

  • Frees you from TA duties. Most universities waive teaching requirements for F31 awardees. Three years of protected research time is the primary benefit beyond the stipend.
  • CV signal that travels. An F31 on your CV tells every reviewer in academia and industry that your work passed NIH peer review during your PhD — before publication, before a degree, before a postdoc. This is rare and valuable.
  • Portability is limited. Unlike the NSF GRFP, the F31 is tied to your institution. If you transfer, you must reapply. Plan accordingly if you are considering a PI or institution change.
  • Target your Institute. NCI, NIMHD, NCATS, NIBIB, NHLBI, NIGMS each have different missions, paylines, and priorities. Submit to the wrong IC and your application lands in front of reviewers who don't value your work. Contact the IC program officer before submitting.
  • Success rate: 20–30% depending on the IC and fiscal year. Check your IC's current payline (posted on their website) before each submission cycle.
  • ORCID required. Effective 2025, your eRA Commons account must be linked to an ORCID iD before submission. Applications missing this are rejected administratively — set this up now at orcid.org.

Eligibility requirements

  • US citizen, national, or lawful permanent resident. F-1 and J-1 visa holders are not eligible. Permanent residents (green card holders) ARE eligible. You self-certify; NIH may request documentation upon award.
  • Best timing: after qualifying exams with preliminary data. There is no formal year requirement, but applications in years 2–4 are strongest. Year 1 is possible but harder to demonstrate research feasibility. Year 5+ raises concerns about whether funding will reach you before graduation.
  • Named faculty sponsor (dissertation advisor) with an active, productive research program and a demonstrable track record of mentoring predoctoral students to completion.
  • 5-year aggregate cap on all NRSA predoctoral support combined (T32 institutional training grant slots + F31). If you've spent time on a T32, that counts against your F31 duration ceiling.
  • One resubmission (A1) allowed. After that, submit as a substantially new application (A0). Many awardees win on A1 — a rejection is the beginning of a revision process, not an end.
  • Cannot be held simultaneously with another federal fellowship that provides overlapping support. Sequential holding is fine and strategically recommended (NSF GRFP → F31 → F32 → K99/R00).

Goals, Preparedness, and Potential Candidate Section

The primary statement from you about who you are, where you've been, and where you're going. Reviewed under Criterion 1. New in 2025: must include a self-assessment of strengths and areas for development.

  1. Background and scientific journey — how you got here. Not a childhood story — your specific scientific experiences, turning points, and the community or problem that drives your research.
  2. Relevant research experience — use the 7-element formula (detailed in the Writing Guide section below). Be specific about your role, results, and what you learned.
  3. Self-assessment of strengths and development needs (required in 2025) — explicitly identify 2–3 skills you still need to develop and show how the training plan addresses each one. Generic statements score poorly. Use myIDP (myidp.sciencecareers.org — free, 30 minutes) to systematically identify your development areas; mentioning that you used it signals career intentionality to reviewers.
  4. Career goals — where are you going after the PhD? What type of scientist, in what sector, with what impact? Connect this directly to the fellowship: why does THIS training plan, with THIS sponsor, lead to THAT career?

Research Training Plan Specific Aims + Research Strategy

Reviewed under Criterion 2. The science must be rigorous and feasible — but the training must also be real. Every experimental aim should connect to a skill you are developing.

  1. Specific Aims page (1 page) — the most-read page of your application. Opens with the problem and gap, states a testable hypothesis, lists 2–3 aims, closes with payoff. See Writing Guide for annotated template.
  2. Research Strategy (6 pages) — Significance (~1 page): why this matters to health and to science. Innovation (~½ page): what is novel about your approach. Approach (~4.5 pages): for each aim, rationale, methods, expected outcomes, pitfalls, and alternative approaches.
  3. Training Plan — a detailed, individualized plan listing specific new skills, named mentors for each skill, courses and workshops, and career milestones with a timeline. Not a list of courses — a genuine development roadmap.
  4. Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) — required section. Name specific RCR courses, workshops, or institutional programs you will complete. This is scored as an additional review criterion.

Sponsor Statement Reviewed under Criterion 3

  • Criterion 3 is scored independently. A famous advisor with a boilerplate letter will score poorly. Reviewers are explicitly instructed not to use lab or institutional prestige as a proxy for training quality.
  • The sponsor statement must describe: (1) a specific, individualized mentoring plan for you — not for all trainees, (2) how they will promote your independence by the end of the fellowship, (3) their track record of previous trainees with names and current positions, (4) the lab's capacity to support your specific project.
  • Ask your advisor to write the letter after reading your draft training plan, so the sponsor statement directly addresses your stated development needs. Misalignment between your self-assessment and your sponsor's letter creates reviewer dissonance.
  • Co-sponsors and consultants are allowed and can strengthen Criterion 3 — use them for skills outside your PI's expertise (e.g., a biostatistics consultant for a specific analysis method).

Reference Letters & Supporting Documents

  • Request letters 6–8 weeks before the deadline. Give writers your draft Candidate section and a brief note on what you hope each letter will address. Letters go directly into eRA Commons — you do not see them.
  • Strong letters describe what you did, not what you are. "She designed and executed the RNA-seq pipeline independently, troubleshot a 6-week sequencing failure, and produced a revised dataset that formed the basis of a first-author paper" beats "She is an exceptional student."
  • Ask for a specific Broader Impacts letter from a mentor who observed your teaching, mentoring, or community work — not just your science. Criterion 1 evaluates your potential as a whole person, not just a researcher.
  • Biosketch: Use NIH FORMS-I format. Personal statement (up to 5 sentences) should connect your background specifically to this fellowship. Include preprints, conference abstracts, posters, and any datasets you have contributed to — not just peer-reviewed publications.
Mistake 1

Writing an R01, not a training award

The F31 is not a small research grant — it's a training fellowship. Reviewers primarily judge whether this experience will develop you into an independent scientist. Every section should answer: "How does this help me grow?" If the training rationale is absent, the score suffers regardless of scientific rigor.

Mistake 2

A generic self-assessment

The 2025 restructuring made self-assessment an explicit requirement under Criterion 1. "I hope to improve my communication skills" is too vague to evaluate. Name which specific techniques, methods, or professional skills you need to develop. Show exactly how the training plan addresses each gap.

Mistake 3

Boilerplate sponsor statement

Criterion 3 is scored independently and reviewer guidance explicitly discourages using lab prestige as a proxy. A sponsor letter describing the research program without a specific, individualized mentoring plan for you will score poorly — even if written by a Nobel laureate.

Mistake 4

Applying to the wrong IC

Each IC has its own mission, payline, and reviewer pool. A health disparities AI proposal scored by a basic science panel will not fare as well as the same proposal at NIMHD. Read the NOFO's Table of IC-Specific Information and call the listed program officer before submitting.

Mistake 5

ORCID not linked to eRA Commons

Since 2025, applications submitted without an ORCID iD linked to your eRA Commons account are rejected at the administrative level — before peer review. Register at orcid.org, then link it in your eRA Commons profile under "Personal Information." Do not leave this for submission day.

Mistake 6

Not planning for the 10–12 month lag

Submission to first payment takes 10–12 months. Submit in August 2026 → earliest funding August 2027. If your current funding runs out before then, you will have a gap. Discuss bridge funding (RA position, T32, advisor's R01) with your advisor before you submit — not after you're awarded.

Resubmission (A1) — how to use reviewer feedback

  • A rejection is not a failure — it is detailed feedback paid for by NIH. Many funded F31 awardees won on their A1. Read your Summary Statement (reviewer comments) within 24 hours of receiving it, then let it sit for a week before drafting your response.
  • The A1 includes a 1-page Introduction (Criterion-specific Introductions are now allowed as of 2025 in some cases — check the specific NOFO). This is the first thing reviewers read. Be direct: "Reviewer 2 noted the training plan lacked specificity. We have revised Section 3 to include named mentors for each of the four identified development areas, specific courses and timelines, and measurable milestones [see page 7]."
  • Address every reviewer concern, including minor ones. Skipping a concern implies you disagreed or ignored it — either interpretation hurts. You can respectfully push back, but do so diplomatically and briefly.
  • Add new preliminary data if you have it. Nothing silences feasibility concerns more effectively than results.
  • If multiple reviewers misunderstood the same section, the writing was unclear — don't just rephrase, restructure the entire section.
  • You get one A1. If still not funded, your next submission must be an A0 (substantially new application). Work with your advisor and grants office on the strategy.

After the A1 — what to do if still not funded

  • Request an appeal only for clear procedural errors (wrong study section, reviewer conflict of interest). Appeals are not for scientific disagreements and are rarely successful.
  • Submit as a new A0 with substantive changes — this is not "the same application with a different cover." Change the title, restructure the aims, incorporate new data, and address the fundamental concerns that caused the previous unfundable score.
  • Change study sections if warranted. If you believe your work was reviewed by the wrong panel, contact the Division of Receipt and Referral (CSR) before your next submission to request appropriate assignment.
  • Consider whether a different IC might be a better fit for your revised proposal — your work may have evolved since the first submission.
  • Reviewers' summary statements are a gift. Keep them. They represent detailed expert feedback on your scientific communication and research plan that you would otherwise never receive. Use them to improve your dissertation work, not just the next application.
Critical Update — January 25, 2025

The 2025 Review Criteria Restructuring

Five old criteria were consolidated into three new ones, effective for all applications submitted January 25, 2025 and later. Many online guides and pre-2025 example essays are built around the wrong criteria structure. Don't follow them uncritically.

🚫 Before Jan 25, 2025 — Five criteria (outdated — do not use)
  1. Fellowship Applicant
  2. Sponsors, Collaborators, and Consultants
  3. Research Training Plan
  4. Training Potential
  5. Institutional Environment and Commitment to Training
✓ Jan 25, 2025 onward — Three criteria (current · use these)
  1. Candidate's Preparedness and Potential
  2. Research Training Plan
  3. Commitment to Candidate, Mentoring, and Training Environment
Scored 1–9 independently
1

Candidate's Preparedness & Potential

  • Do past experiences prepare you for this training plan? Reviewers consider your stage — they do not expect you to have a postdoc CV. Opportunity context matters.
  • Do your statements — and your sponsor's and your letters — show scientific understanding, creativity, curiosity, resourcefulness, and drive?
  • Can you benefit from and complete this plan, then successfully transition to the next career stage in biomedical research?
  • New 2025 element: an explicit self-assessment of your strengths and specific development areas. Generic self-assessment scores poorly — be specific and honest.
Scored 1–9 independently
2

Research Training Plan

  • Is the research project rigorous and feasible? Will completing it develop you as a research scientist — not just produce data?
  • Do the training activities address your identified development needs in a realistic, appropriate way with clear milestones?
  • Are sponsor, environment, facilities, and resources adequate for the proposed plan? No preliminary data formally required — but science must have a sound foundation.
  • NIH reviewers examine this criterion for training quality, not just scientific quality. A rigorous science plan with a vague training plan will not score well.
Scored 1–9 independently
3

Commitment to Candidate, Mentoring & Environment

  • Does the sponsor present a strong, specific mentoring plan appropriate to your individual needs and goals? Generalities are red flags — reviewers notice template letters.
  • Is the sponsor's and institution's commitment sufficient and aligned with the training plan?
  • Critical 2025 change: reviewer guidance explicitly discourages using the sponsor's reputation or the lab's prestige as a proxy for training quality. A famous advisor without a specific plan will not rescue a weak Criterion 3.
📌 Overall Impact Score: Reviewers assign a single Overall Impact Score (1–9, best to worst) reflecting the likelihood that the fellowship will enhance your potential for a productive independent research career in a health-related field. The three criteria inform but do not mechanically determine this score. Only the top ~50% of reviewed applications receive scores — the rest are triaged without discussion. All three criteria must be strong enough to avoid being the reason an application is triaged.
Writing Guide — All Content Inline

Writing the NIH Fellowship Application

Complete inline guidance — no external links required. Every framework, example, and annotation is on this page. Examples are written in a computational health / AI context relevant to Meharry SACS students — adapt the language to your specific research area.

Recommended structure

  1. Opening: problem and your role in it1–2 sentences — Start with a clear statement of the scientific problem and your connection to it. Not a childhood story. Not "I am passionate." A specific, orienting statement.
  2. Scientific journey and formative experiences~½ page — The turning points that led you to this research. What have you observed, experienced, or learned that makes this problem urgent for you specifically?
  3. Relevant research experience (7-element formula)~¾ page — One structured paragraph per major research experience. See the formula below.
  4. Self-assessment of strengths and development needs~½ page — Required as of 2025. Be honest and specific: 2–3 skills you have, 2–3 you are developing, and how this fellowship addresses each gap.
  5. Career goals~¼ page — Where are you going after the PhD? Be direct: faculty position at an R1/HBCU, industry research scientist, government lab, health policy. Connect your goal to the training plan.

7-Element Research Experience Formula

Each major research experience deserves one structured paragraph using all seven elements. This is the most reliably strong format for describing your research background.

1

Why this lab / what drew you here

The scientific question that attracted you to this project or mentor — not "I was recruited" but what scientific problem made you say yes.

e.g., "I joined Dr. [X]'s lab to investigate whether federated learning could close the performance gap between majority-population AI models and minority patient data."
2

Your specific role — not "assisted with"

Name your actual contribution. "I designed the differential privacy implementation" not "I helped with the privacy component."

3

A concrete result with a number

Quantify. "The model achieved 94.2% of centralized accuracy with ε=0.8 differential privacy." Reviewers remember numbers.

4

What you produced (paper, tool, dataset)

"This work led to a first-author submission to [Journal]" or "I built a publicly released Python library used by 3 external labs."

5

How you shared it

Presentations, talks, poster sessions, preprints. "I presented preliminary findings at the 2026 IEEE S&P symposium."

6

A specific skill you gained

"This experience established my expertise in differential privacy theory and federated optimization under non-IID data distributions."

7

How it shaped your direction

One sentence connecting this experience to your dissertation and career goal. Thread the narrative through all experiences.

Opening Paragraph — Weak vs. Strong
✗ Weak — Do Not Model

"As someone who has always been interested in computers and healthcare, I am applying for the NIH F31 fellowship to support my research in machine learning. I grew up watching family members struggle with health problems, which inspired me to pursue a career in research. I am passionate about using artificial intelligence to help people. My time at Meharry Medical College has been very rewarding and I believe this fellowship will help me achieve my goals as a researcher."

Problems: Vague opening. "Passionate" is a red flag. No specific scientific problem stated. No preliminary data or achievements mentioned. Could describe any student in any field.
✓ Strong — Adapt This Pattern

"Cardiovascular disease kills Black Americans at 1.3× the rate of white Americans — a disparity that widens every year clinical AI models are trained on majority-population data that systematically excludes minority patients. As a doctoral researcher at Meharry Medical College's School of Applied Computational Sciences, I have spent three years building federated machine learning systems designed to correct this: privacy-preserving distributed architectures that learn from patient records across minority-serving institutions without centralizing sensitive data. My preliminary results show that a federated model trained across three HBCUs achieves 94% of centralized model accuracy while meeting differential privacy guarantees — demonstrating both technical feasibility and a principled approach to AI health equity. I am applying for the F31 to complete my dissertation, publish these findings, and build the independent research career I am already establishing."

Strengths: Opens with the problem and stakes. Shows specific research, specific results, and a specific number. Makes the connection to health equity explicit. The fellowship purpose is clear and concrete.
Research Experience Paragraph — Weak vs. Strong
✗ Weak

"During my second year, I worked in a machine learning lab that studies healthcare applications. I helped with various projects involving data analysis and learned a lot about research methods. I also attended weekly lab meetings and contributed to team discussions. The experience was very valuable for my development as a researcher."

Problems: No specific project named. "Helped with" is passive and vague. No results. No numbers. No product. No skill named. Could describe anyone.
✓ Strong (7-element formula applied)

"In my second year, I joined Dr. [Advisor]'s Distributed AI for Health Equity Lab to investigate whether federated learning could match centralized model accuracy while preserving data sovereignty at minority-serving institutions [element 1: why this lab]. My specific contribution was designing the differential privacy implementation and evaluating the accuracy-privacy tradeoff across three hospital datasets [element 2: specific role]. Over 14 months, I developed a pipeline that achieved 94.2% of centralized model accuracy with a formal ε=0.8 guarantee [element 3: result with number], resulting in a first-author manuscript under review at Nature Communications [element 4: product]. I presented preliminary findings at the 2026 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy [element 5: dissemination]. This experience established my technical expertise in differential privacy theory and federated optimization under heterogeneous data distributions [element 6: skill], and confirmed that building equitable AI for underserved health systems is exactly the research career I am committed to [element 7: direction]."

The Specific Aims page is the single most important page of your application. Reviewers form their preliminary score here and many decide whether to read the rest carefully. Every sentence must earn its place.

Annotated Specific Aims Template
Sentence 1–2: The problem and its stakes. One sentence for the health relevance. One for the human cost. Do not start with background — start with the problem.

"Cardiometabolic diseases impose a 1.4× higher mortality burden on Black Americans compared to non-Hispanic whites [CDC 2024], yet clinical prediction models built on majority-population data perform 23% worse when applied to minority patients — a well-documented disparity driven by systematic training data exclusion [Smith et al. 2023, Jones et al. 2024]."

Sentence 3–4: The specific gap in knowledge. What does the field currently NOT know or cannot currently do? This gap must be real — cite the literature that defines it.

"Federated learning can in principle include minority-serving institutions by removing the requirement to centralize patient data — but whether it actually improves prediction equity, and under what conditions, remains uncharacterized."

Long-term goal + overall objective. Two sentences. Career vision first, then what THIS fellowship achieves.

"The long-term goal of this research program is to establish federated AI as the standard architecture for equitable clinical decision support in underserved health systems. The overall objective of this fellowship is to characterize the conditions under which privacy-preserving federated learning reduces racial prediction disparities in cardiovascular risk assessment, and to build validated open-source tools deployable at HBCUs and federally qualified health centers."

Central hypothesis. One testable, falsifiable statement. NOT "The goal of this project is to…" NOT a question. A hypothesis.

"We hypothesize that a privacy-preserving federated learning framework co-designed with three Historically Black-serving health systems will (1) match centralized AI accuracy within 5% while meeting formal differential privacy guarantees and (2) demonstrably reduce prediction error disparities across racial subgroups."

2–3 Specific Aims — one sentence each. Each aim should be largely independent. Avoid "Aim 2 depends on Aim 1 succeeding" — if Aim 1 is high-risk, the whole application looks fragile.

"Aim 1. Characterize the accuracy-equity tradeoff in federated vs. centralized cardiovascular risk models across three minority-serving hospital networks. Aim 2. Identify the data heterogeneity conditions under which federated learning most reduces racial prediction gaps, using systematic sensitivity analysis and synthetic data augmentation. Aim 3. Develop and externally validate a differential-privacy–preserving federated learning toolkit optimized for low-resource HBCU clinical environments."

Payoff paragraph. What will the field know, and what will change, when this work is complete? Connect to the gap you identified. End strong.

"Completion of these aims will produce the first systematic evidence base for federated learning as an equity-improving architecture in cardiovascular AI, along with open-source tools ready for deployment in under-resourced clinical settings. These contributions will directly address the mechanisms driving AI-based health disparities, advancing NIH's commitment to reducing health disparities through precision medicine and providing a generalizable framework for equitable AI across other clinical conditions and underrepresented populations."

Opening Paragraph — Weak vs. Strong
✗ Weak Specific Aims opening

"Cardiovascular disease is a major health problem in the United States affecting millions of Americans. Many researchers have studied this topic. However, there is still a need for better prediction tools. We propose to study machine learning methods to predict heart disease risk in different populations. This research will be important for improving healthcare outcomes."

Problems: Opens with a fact that needs no citation. "There is still a need" is not a specific gap. "Different populations" is not defined. No hypothesis. Reviewer has learned nothing by the end of this paragraph.
✓ Strong Specific Aims opening

"Cardiometabolic diseases impose a 1.4× higher mortality burden on Black Americans compared to non-Hispanic whites [CDC 2024]. Clinical AI models built on majority-population data — which systematically exclude minority patients — perform 23% worse in this population [Smith et al. 2023], a disparity that worsens every year AI is deployed without correction. Federated learning, which allows AI models to train across decentralized datasets without transferring raw patient records, offers a principled solution: it can incorporate clinical data from minority-serving institutions blocked from centralized training by privacy constraints. Yet whether federated learning actually improves prediction equity — and under what data heterogeneity conditions — remains uncharacterized. This proposal will fill that gap."

Strengths: Leads with stakes and numbers. Names the specific mechanism (exclusion of minority-serving institutions). Identifies the gap precisely. Sets up the hypothesis cleanly. The reviewer knows exactly what problem this addresses.
Hypothesis Statements — Right vs. Wrong
✗ Not a hypothesis

"The goal of this research is to develop federated learning tools for healthcare AI that work across diverse hospital systems."

This is a statement of intent, not a hypothesis. There is nothing to disprove here.
✓ Testable hypothesis

"We hypothesize that a federated model trained across three HBCU-affiliated hospital networks under ε=1.0 differential privacy will achieve cardiovascular risk prediction accuracy within 5% of a centralized model while demonstrably reducing racial prediction disparity."

This can be falsified. It makes specific predictions (5% accuracy gap, reduced disparity). Reviewers can evaluate whether the proposed experiments would actually test it.

Significance — the argument for importance

~1 page. The purpose of Significance is not to review the literature — it is to make the case that THIS specific gap, addressed NOW, will change something important. Structure it as an argument, not a history.

✗ Weak Significance

"AI in healthcare is a growing field with many important applications. Researchers have developed many algorithms for predicting cardiovascular risk. However, more research is still needed on diverse populations. This research will contribute to this growing field."

✓ Strong Significance

"The FDA has approved 521 AI/ML-based clinical decision tools as of 2024 — 98% trained on datasets that include <12% Black patients [FDA AI Report 2024]. The resulting prediction gap is not theoretical: in three independent validation studies, models trained without minority data produce 23% higher false-negative rates for cardiovascular risk in Black patients [citations]. This is a mechanism by which technological progress actively amplifies the disparity it was deployed to reduce. Federated learning eliminates the architectural barrier (centralized data sharing) that has kept minority-serving institutions out of AI training pipelines — but its equity impact has not been systematically characterized. The proposed research will produce the evidence base needed for FDA-relevant recommendations on equitable AI architecture requirements."

Innovation — the novel element

~½ page. You do not need to invent a new technique. Combining existing methods in a new context, asking a genuinely new question, or applying established methods to a critically undercharacterized problem all qualify as innovative.

  • Lead with the specific novelty: "This proposal is innovative because it is the first to systematically characterize how differential privacy parameters interact with data heterogeneity to affect racial prediction equity in federated cardiovascular AI."
  • Avoid vague innovation language: "This research uses cutting-edge AI methods" tells reviewers nothing. Name the specific technique, the specific application context, and the specific prior work your approach moves beyond.
  • If your approach is inherently incremental (a next step in your field, not a leap), be honest about that and frame the innovation as the question you are asking, not the method you are using.
  • Three valid types of innovation for SACS students: (1) a new algorithm or implementation, (2) a new application of existing methods to an undercharacterized population or setting, (3) a new scientific question that existing methods can now answer for the first time.

Approach — for each aim, use this sub-structure

Rationale: Why is this aim necessary? What does answering it enable? One short paragraph that connects back to your hypothesis and the gap.
Design and methods: What will you do, in what order, with what data, using what analytical approach? Be specific — reviewers look for whether a trainee understands their own methods.
Expected outcomes and success criteria: "This aim will succeed if [specific measurable criterion]." Reviewers are scored on feasibility — explicit success criteria demonstrate scientific maturity.
Potential pitfalls: One paragraph identifying the most likely failure mode of this aim. Not a confession of weakness — a demonstration that you have thought it through.
Alternative approaches: If the primary approach fails, what will you do? This paragraph is scored by reviewers as a test of scientific maturity. Have at least one credible alternative for each high-risk step.
Preliminary data (include wherever you have it): A figure, a pilot result, a feasibility calculation. Even a power calculation grounded in pilot data is better than no preliminary data at all.
📌 Page limits and density: The F31 Research Strategy is 6 pages — not 12 like an R01. This forces prioritization. If you have 3 aims and 4.5 pages of Approach, that is roughly 1.5 pages per aim. Every sentence must carry information. Cut all filler. Remove every sentence that does not change the reviewer's understanding of what you will do or why it matters.

The Training Plan is the section that most clearly distinguishes the F31 from an R-series grant. It must demonstrate that this fellowship will produce a genuinely different scientist by the end of the award period. A weak training plan will drag down an otherwise strong application.

Training Plan structure and required elements

  1. Specific skills you will acquire — list 3–5 skills you do NOT currently have. These must be genuinely new, not refinements of existing expertise. Name the technique and the plan for acquiring it: who will teach you, in what format, over what timeline.
  2. Career development activities — courses (named, with schedule), workshops, seminar series, journal clubs, and any external professional development. Reviewers notice if all activities are "attending lab meetings."
  3. Mentorship plan — primary sponsor and any co-sponsors or external consultants for specific skills. How often do you meet? How does the sponsor plan to promote your independence over the fellowship period?
  4. Milestones with a timeline — a table or list with dates: qualifying exam (if not yet done), first paper submission, dissertation chapter completion, conference presentations, postdoc application start, anticipated defense date.
  5. Career goals and path forward — where specifically are you going after the PhD? Faculty position, industry, government lab? Connect this goal to the training plan: how does each activity you have listed serve this specific career objective?
  6. Responsible Conduct of Research plan — required. Name specific courses and workshops at Meharry or the broader consortium. "RCR training will be completed as required" is not acceptable.

Strong vs. weak training plan language

✗ Weak

"I will develop skills in machine learning and data science through coursework and lab meetings. I will also attend seminars and conferences to build my professional network. I plan to mentor undergraduate students and participate in outreach activities. Upon completion of this fellowship, I will be well-prepared for a career in biomedical research."

No specific skills named. No named courses. No timeline. No specific conferences. Career goal is generic. Reviewers cannot evaluate this plan.
✓ Strong

"I currently lack expertise in (1) causal inference methods for observational health data and (2) formal verification of differential privacy guarantees. I will address gap (1) through CS-8420 (Causal Inference for Health Data, Meharry, Spring 2027) with Dr. [Name], and through a 3-month reading group I will organize with Dr. [Co-mentor] at Vanderbilt's biostatistics department. I will address gap (2) through enrollment in the Coursera Cryptography course series (Months 1–4) and a direct mentored project with Dr. [Co-sponsor], who will dedicate 1 hour/week to verifying my proofs. Milestones: Aim 1 data collection complete by Month 6; first manuscript submitted by Month 12; Aim 2 analysis complete Month 18; dissertation defense Month 36. I will present at ICLR 2027 and AMIA 2027. I will apply to faculty positions beginning Month 28, targeting R1 institutions with health informatics or computer science departments."

📌 The RCR Plan: Minimum content — name the formal RCR course you will take (with semester), describe one additional activity (e.g., monthly lab ethics discussions, journal club on data integrity), and confirm that any human subjects or vertebrate animal training required for your research will be completed before data collection. Meharry's IRB office and graduate school can advise on what is available locally.

What a strong sponsor statement contains

Criterion 3 is scored independently. Brief your advisor on exactly what reviewers are looking for — most PIs have never read the F31 reviewer guidance document and write letters that would have scored well under the old criteria but miss what Criterion 3 now requires.

  • An individualized mentoring plan specifically for you — not a description of how the lab mentors trainees in general. Named activities, named co-mentors, named timelines. "I meet with all my students weekly" is generic. "I will meet with [student] weekly to review progress on Aim 1 analysis through Month 6, then transition to biweekly as they develop independence" is specific.
  • How the sponsor will promote independence — reviewers specifically look for a plan for how the student will develop autonomous scientific thinking, not just execute the PI's research program. What decisions will the student make independently? When?
  • Confirmed resources — access to specific datasets, computing infrastructure, collaborators, and lab equipment needed for the proposed aims. If a resource requires formal access (a hospital dataset, a federal computing cluster), confirm that access is in place or describe the specific steps to obtain it.
  • Track record of previous trainees — names and current positions of 3–5 former PhD students or postdocs the sponsor has mentored to completion. "Several students have gone on to faculty positions" is not enough. "Dr. [name] (PhD 2022, now asst. professor at [university]); Dr. [name] (PhD 2023, now research scientist at [company])." Reviewers look at this list.
  • How this project fits within but is not simply a subsection of the sponsor's R01 — the student's dissertation must be the student's own work. The letter should explain how the F31 project connects to the lab's broader research while constituting the student's independent contribution.

How to ask your advisor for the right letter

Your advisor writes what they know. It is your job to give them what they need. Schedule a meeting before they write a single word.

  • Send them your draft Candidate section and Training Plan before the meeting. Ask them to read your self-assessment, because their letter should directly address the development needs you identified.
  • Share a brief memo explaining the 2025 review criteria structure — specifically that Criterion 3 is now scored separately, and that reviewers are told not to use lab prestige as a proxy. Frame this as helping them write the most effective letter, not critiquing their typical approach.
  • Provide a short bullet list of what a strong Criterion 3 letter includes (individualized plan, how you'll build independence, confirmed resources, previous trainee track record). Offer to draft this section if they would find a starting point helpful.
  • If you have a co-sponsor or external consultant, coordinate so their letter explicitly addresses the specific skill they are providing that your primary advisor cannot. Letters that say the same things are wasted pages.
  • Give them 6–8 weeks. Check in politely 4 weeks before the deadline to confirm they are on track.
⚠ If your advisor cannot write a specific letter: Some PIs are genuinely unable to write strong F31 sponsor letters because they haven't supervised to completion, don't have adequate lab resources, or simply don't know how. This is a signal — either work with them intensively on letter preparation, or discuss adding a co-sponsor who can provide what the primary sponsor cannot. A weak Criterion 3 score is extremely hard to overcome, even with excellent Criteria 1 and 2.
Inside the Review Room

What Funded vs. Unfunded Applications Sound Like

Understanding how reviewers think and write helps you write for your audience. These examples are composites drawn from publicly available reviewer guidance, published summary statements, and application outcome patterns — not verbatim quotes from any specific review.

🌟 Impact Score 1–2 — What reviewers write about funded applications
"The candidate demonstrates exceptional promise for this career stage. The application includes two first-author publications in high-impact venues and three conference presentations, indicating strong productivity and communication skills. Importantly, the candidate's self-assessment is honest and specific — they identify a clear gap in causal inference methodology and describe a named course and co-mentor arrangement to address it, which aligns precisely with the training plan. The research proposal is scientifically rigorous with a well-defined, testable hypothesis and achievable aims that do not depend serially on each other. The sponsor's letter is outstanding: rather than describing the laboratory program, it articulates month-by-month milestones, names three former PhD trainees now in faculty positions, and explicitly commits to transitioning the candidate to independent decision-making by Year 2. The health equity angle of this research is genuine and well-motivated, and the plan for deploying results at minority-serving institutions is credible and specific."
Why this scored well: Named achievements with specificity. Self-assessment aligned with training plan. Testable hypothesis. Non-serial aims. Sponsor letter individualized with track record. Health equity impact connected to actual deployment plan.
⚠ Impact Score 4–5 — What reviewers write about unfunded applications
"While the candidate shows potential, several aspects of the application raise concerns. The self-assessment states the candidate 'hopes to improve scientific writing and presentation skills' but these are not addressed in the training plan — the disconnect between the stated development need and the plan to address it weakens Criterion 1. Aim 2 proposes to 'explore alternative architectures' without specifying what comparisons will be made or by what criteria a selection will occur, making feasibility difficult to assess. Most significantly, the sponsor statement describes the laboratory's research program in detail but does not identify how this candidate's individual training needs will be addressed differently from other trainees. The Broader Impacts section — while well-intentioned — reads as an afterthought: a single paragraph stating the candidate 'plans to mentor underrepresented students' with no timeline, no numbers, no institutional plan. The reviewers encourage the candidate to resubmit with a more individualized training plan and a sponsor letter that directly addresses Criterion 3."
How to fix this for A1: Align self-assessment gaps with specific training plan activities. Add explicit success criteria to each aim. Work with sponsor on writing a specific mentoring plan. Expand Broader Impacts with numbers, timeline, and named programs.
📌 How to get your own reviewer feedback: Your Summary Statement (the official record of reviewer comments) is available in your eRA Commons account approximately 30 days after the study section meeting. Read it regardless of your score. If you receive a score that is near the funding line but not funded (a "near miss"), request a meeting with your IC program officer — they can often tell you which criterion held you back and whether a resubmission is advisable for your specific situation.
NIH Intramural — Highest Stipend

GPP — Graduate Partnerships Program

Conduct your dissertation research in an NIH Intramural Research Program (IRP) laboratory while remaining enrolled at your home university — which may be anywhere in the world. The highest predoctoral stipend available through NIH, with health insurance fully covered. The only major NIH mechanism open to international students.

Individual Partnership

You're already enrolled — you find an NIH PI and create a research agreement between your university and NIH.

  • DeadlineOpen year-round. No central deadline. Apply when you have identified a mentor and your graduate program has approved the arrangement.
  • CitizenshipUS citizens, permanent residents, AND foreign nationals. Students at universities worldwide are eligible — this is the only major NIH fellowship with no citizenship restriction.
  • Eligible fieldsBiology, engineering, epidemiology, psychology, mathematics, chemistry, pharmaceutical sciences, nursing, physics, computer science, bioinformatics, and other health-related fields. Basic and translational research both supported.
  • First stepBrowse NIH IRP labs at irp.nih.gov and contact PIs directly. Reach out to 5–10 PIs whose work aligns with your research. No centralized application exists before a PI agrees to host you.
  • Background checkFederal background check required before accessing any NIH campus. Review requirements early at training.nih.gov/federal-background-check — allow 4–8 weeks.

Institutional Partnership

Your university has a formal agreement with NIH — you enroll in a PhD program designed around NIH research from the start.

  • DeadlineApplications typically open August and close December annually. Check with specific partner institutions for exact dates — they vary by school and IC.
  • CitizenshipMost institutional partnerships require US citizenship or permanent residency. International eligibility varies by partnership — confirm with each specific program.
  • Partner schoolsGeorgetown, University of Maryland, Virginia Tech, Brown, and 30+ others have formal partnerships. See training.nih.gov/gpp for current list.
  • Apply bothYou apply to the university's PhD program AND the NIH GPP Application Center simultaneously. Some partnerships require both applications in the same cycle.
  • For MeharryMeharry is not currently a formal institutional partner. Individual partnerships are the path for current Meharry SACS students. Contact your SRO about establishing an institutional partnership.

NIH Intramural — Elite Dual-Institution

NIH Oxford-Cambridge Scholars Program

An accelerated dual-degree PhD program splitting dissertation research between an NIH Intramural Research Program laboratory and the University of Oxford or Cambridge. Pure research — no coursework. Approximately 4 years to degree. US citizens only. Annual application deadline approximately December 1.

What the program provides

  • PhD from Oxford or Cambridge with dissertation research conducted partly at NIH IRP. The degree is awarded by the UK institution; the research happens at both sites.
  • Stipend: At or above the NIH stipend table for the appropriate experience level — typically $35,000–$40,000+ depending on the IC and experience. Health insurance and housing assistance at NIH campus.
  • Pure research — no coursework requirement. OxCam is for students who want to spend 100% of their time on research, mentored by two PIs (one at NIH, one at Oxford or Cambridge).
  • Accelerated completion: Students typically complete the joint PhD in ~4 years — faster than average US doctoral programs.
  • Access to both research environments: NIH's world-class biomedical infrastructure in Bethesda and the UK university's research culture and networks.
  • International network: The OxCam community is small and connected — alumni hold senior positions in global health, academia, and policy.

Eligibility and application

  • US citizens only — unlike the GPP individual partnership, the OxCam Scholars Program does not accept permanent residents or international students.
  • Apply before starting a PhD program — this is an entry-level program. You must not yet have matriculated into a doctoral program elsewhere. Applications from students who have started a PhD are not accepted.
  • Annual deadline approximately December 1 — verify the exact date for the current cycle at oxcam.gpp.nih.gov. The application includes: research statement, CV, three letters of recommendation, GRE scores (check current requirements — may vary by year), and a statement of research interests aligned with NIH IRP.
  • Find an NIH PI first. The most competitive applicants contact NIH IRP labs aligned with their research interests before the formal deadline. Many PIsselecttheir OxCam students directly from prospective applicants they meet at conferences or who cold-contact them with a compelling research pitch.
  • Research areas: All biomedical, computational, and behavioral sciences supported by NIH IRP — CS, bioinformatics, structural biology, neuroscience, and many others.

T32 — Institutional Research Training Grant

Ask your program director · You apply through your institution

  • T32 grants are held by institutions, not individual students. Your program director applies — you benefit by being named a T32 trainee, receiving the NRSA stipend ($29,364/yr predoc) and tuition coverage.
  • Ask your graduate program director whether Meharry holds any T32 training grants relevant to your field. If Meharry does not hold one, nearby institutions (Vanderbilt, Fisk, UT Health) may accept Meharry trainees through consortium agreements.

The National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities has the most direct mission alignment for Meharry students. Work addressing racial disparities in clinical AI performance, algorithmic bias, or health technology equity is core NIMHD territory. Meharry's HBCU context and community-connected research are explicit assets in this IC. Check nimhd.nih.gov/about/strategic-plan/ for current priority areas before framing your proposal.

NIBIB

Biomedical AI · Privacy-Preserving Systems · Health Informatics

The National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering funds computational tools, AI systems, and digital health technologies. Differential privacy implementations, secure multiparty computation for health data, federated optimization algorithms, and privacy-preserving clinical decision support all have strong NIBIB alignment. NIBIB funds methods development even when clinical deployment is future work.

NCI

Cancer Data Science · Oncology AI · Computational Oncology

NCI's Center for Biomedical Informatics and Information Technology (CBIIT) actively funds AI/ML in cancer. If your research touches cancer screening, cancer disparities, or oncology data science — even as a secondary focus — NCI is worth contacting. The NCI payline is currently more favorable than some other ICs. NCI also offers specific F99/K00 programs for PhD students targeting cancer research careers.

NHLBI

Cardiometabolic Disparities · Cardiovascular AI

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute is directly relevant for cardiometabolic health disparities research. NHLBI has invested significantly in AI for cardiovascular risk prediction, atrial fibrillation detection, and heart failure management — and has specific programs addressing disparities in these outcomes. Meharry's clinical environment and patient population make cardiovascular AI a natural fit.

NHGRI

Bioinformatics · Genomics · Computational Biology

The National Human Genome Research Institute funds bioinformatics, computational genomics, and genome-to-phenotype AI. If your SACS dissertation touches genomics, electronic health records linked to biobanks, or population genetics of underrepresented groups, NHGRI is your target. NHGRI has a strong interest in increasing diversity of genomic data representation.

Month-by-Month Plan — August 8 Target

F31 Application Timeline

For an August 8 submission. Shift all dates by ±4 months for April or December cycles. The 10–12 month lag between submission and funding means August 2026 submission → earliest funding August 2027.

8–12 mo before
Strategy

Identify your IC and call the program officer

Search NIH Reporter (reporter.nih.gov) for recently funded F31s in your field at your target IC. Identify the program officer listed in the NOFO's Table of IC-Specific Information. Email to introduce yourself and request 15 minutes to discuss fit. This conversation shapes your proposal framing.

6 mo before
Critical

Write your Specific Aims page — start here

Your Specific Aims page is the foundation of everything. Write it first. Share it with your advisor. Revise. Share with a committee member. Revise again. Everything else in the application must align with what the Aims page promises.

5 mo before
Request letters

Request sponsor statement and reference letters now

Give your advisor your aims page and schedule a meeting to discuss what a strong Criterion 3 letter requires. Request 3–4 reference letters with your aims page and a memo describing what you hope each will address. Give writers 4–5 months — they are writing multiple letters simultaneously.

4 mo before
Register

Set up eRA Commons and link your ORCID iD

If you don't have an eRA Commons account, create one now. Link your ORCID iD under "Personal Information" — applications submitted without this linkage are rejected administratively. Register at orcid.org if needed. Contact Meharry's SRO to ensure institutional registration is complete.

3 mo before
Draft

Complete first drafts of all sections

Candidate section, Research Strategy (Significance, Innovation, Approach), Training Plan, and RCR plan. Write all components in parallel — they must tell a coherent story. The Training Plan must directly address the development needs you name in your self-assessment.

6–8 wks before
Revise

Revision cycles — aim for 3 to 4

Share with your PI, thesis committee, and a recent F31 awardee in your field if possible. Read the 2025 review criteria again and verify each criterion is addressed explicitly. Run the Common Mistakes checklist. Verify your ORCID linkage is active.

2–3 wks before
Internal deadline

Submit to Meharry's Sponsored Research Office

SRO requires completed applications 1–2 weeks before NIH's deadline for institutional review and submission. Confirm their exact internal deadline at the start of your process — not the week before. All sections including biosketch and institutional certifications must be final at SRO submission.

August 8
NIH Deadline

Submit via Grants.gov by 5:00 PM Eastern

SRO submits on your behalf. Submit 48+ hours early — system traffic is heavy near deadlines. Confirm receipt of a Grants.gov tracking number. Verify in eRA Commons that all reference letters have been submitted. The letter system closes 5–7 business days after the application deadline — send reminders to your letter writers now.

Oct/Nov review
Review

Study section meets — Summary Statement posted ~30 days later

You will receive your Summary Statement in eRA Commons approximately 30 days after the study section meeting. Read all reviewer comments carefully regardless of your score. They represent detailed expert feedback you would otherwise never receive. Keep them for A1 preparation or future applications.

~Aug 2027
Award

Notice of Award — earliest funding start

If funded, you receive a Notice of Award (NoA) in eRA Commons. If not funded but scored within 10 percentile points of the payline, contact your IC program officer to ask about the possibility of administrative supplements or your A1 strategy. If triaged (no score), the application needs substantial revision before resubmission.

NIH explicitly encourages pre-submission contact. Most applicants never make this call. It is one of the highest-leverage 20 minutes of your application process.

Ask your advisor for introductions, or search NIH Reporter for funded F31s at comparable institutions and email the student author.
