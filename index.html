Decision Guide

Which Program Is Right for You?

Answer the questions below to identify your best starting point. Most students should pursue multiple programs sequentially — you cannot hold them simultaneously.

If you are in a single PhD program

PhD-Only Path

The F31 is your primary target. Apply in years 2–4, after passing qualifying exams. If you're in years 3–5 and can articulate a postdoc plan, also consider the F99/K00. Both require US citizenship or permanent residency.

➜ F31 ➜ F99/K00
If you are in an MD/PhD or dual-degree program

Dual-Degree Path

Apply for the F30, designed specifically for MD/PhD students. Must apply within 48 months of matriculation and no later than 24 months into your dissertation research. Your program must have a clear integration of clinical and research training.

➜ F30
If you want to conduct dissertation research at NIH

NIH Intramural Path

The Graduate Partnerships Program (GPP) lets you do your dissertation research in an NIH lab while remaining enrolled at your home university. The stipend is substantially higher than the F31. International students (J-1) may be eligible — unlike the F31.

➜ GPP
If your advisor has an active NIH grant

Diversity Supplement Path

Your PI can apply for a diversity supplement to their existing NIH award, adding you as a funded trainee. This is often faster and more accessible than an individual fellowship. You must be from an underrepresented group. Your PI initiates the application.

➜ Diversity Supplement
If you are an international student

International Student Path

The F31, F30, and F99/K00 are limited to US citizens and permanent residents. However, the GPP is open to international students on J-1 visas. The diversity supplement may also be available depending on your background and your PI's eligibility.

➜ GPP ➜ Diversity Supp.
Can you hold multiple awards?

Sequential Strategy

You cannot hold an NRSA fellowship (F31, F30) simultaneously with the F99/K00. But you can hold them sequentially: F31 first, then use F99/K00 for your final years and postdoc. Apply for the NSF GRFP early (before or during year 1), then the F31 in years 2–3.

Primary Fellowship

F31 — Ruth L. Kirschstein Predoctoral Fellowship

The most common NIH fellowship for PhD students. Funds dissertation research under a named faculty sponsor. Having an F31 on your CV signals to postdoc supervisors and search committees that your work survived independent peer review before you finished your degree.

Key facts

  • FOA: PA-25-422 (Parent F31). Verify current active FOA at grants.nih.gov before beginning your application.
  • Frees you from TA duties — the primary benefit beyond the stipend is protected research time. Many universities waive TA requirements for F31 awardees.
  • Success rate: 20–30% depending on the NIH Institute or Center (IC). Varies by field and payline. Check your target IC's website for their current payline.
  • Portability: The F31 is tied to your institution. If you transfer, you must submit a new application. (Unlike the NSF GRFP, which is fully portable.)
  • Cannot hold concurrently with another federally sponsored fellowship that provides a stipend or duplicates provisions.
  • Resubmission allowed: If not funded on first try, you get one resubmission (A1). Many successful awardees won on A1.
  • Review criteria revised January 2025: New FORMS-I application format. Check current instructions if applying for the first time.
  • Target your IC carefully: NCI for cancer, NIMHD for health disparities, NCATS for translational/data science, NIBIB for biomedical imaging/engineering, NHGRI for genomics. Each IC has its own payline and priorities.

Eligibility requirements

  • US citizenship or permanent residency required at time of award. Non-citizen nationals are also eligible. F-1, J-1, H-1B visa holders are not eligible. No exceptions.
  • Enrolled full-time in a research doctoral program (PhD, MD/PhD, or equivalent) in biomedical, behavioral, or clinical sciences at a domestic institution.
  • Best timing: years 2–4 of PhD. Most successful applicants have passed their qualifying exam and have preliminary data. Applying in year 1 is possible but harder. Applying after year 4 is risky — reviewers may question whether you need it to finish.
  • Named faculty sponsor required — your dissertation advisor with an active research program and a track record of mentoring predoctoral students. Co-sponsors are allowed and can strengthen the application.
  • Aggregate NRSA support limit: 5 years total predoctoral support counting T32 slots and F31 combined. Track any T32 support received.
  • Each NIH Institute has its own participation — not all ICs fund all research areas. Verify your target IC is participating and accepting applications.
  • One resubmission allowed (A1). After that, submit as a substantially revised new application (A0).

Specific Aims page 1 page — most critical

The single most important page of your application. Reviewers form their first impression here and many decide on a preliminary score before finishing it. Use every sentence deliberately.

  1. Opening paragraph (3–4 sentences) — establish the problem and its significance to human health. State the gap in current knowledge. Be direct — reviewers see 15–25 applications in one sitting.
  2. Long-term goal & overall objective — your career vision and what this fellowship specifically achieves. One sentence each.
  3. Central hypothesis — a clear, testable statement. Not a question — a hypothesis.
  4. 2–3 specific aims — one sentence of approach for each. Avoid more than 3. Avoid interdependent aims where Aim 2 fails if Aim 1 fails.
  5. Payoff paragraph — what will the field know when this work is done? Why does this matter? Close with a compelling statement of expected impact.

Research Strategy 6 pages

  1. Significance (~1 page) — why does this research matter? What changes when you're done? Don't write a literature review — write a focused argument for why this specific gap needs filling. Tie to human health outcomes.
  2. Innovation (~½ page) — what is novel about your approach? This does not require a new technique: combining existing methods in a new context or studying an unexplored question counts. Be specific.
  3. Approach (~4.5 pages) — for each aim: rationale, experimental design, methods, expected outcomes, potential pitfalls, and alternative approaches. Include preliminary data — reviewers want evidence you can execute this project.
The F31 gets 6 pages for Research Strategy — not 12 like an R01. Every sentence must earn its place. Proposing too much is the #1 critique on F31 Approach sections.

Training Plan & Career Goals

Where F31 differs most from an R-series grant. Reviewers score training as heavily as research. A strong Research Strategy with a vague Training Plan will still score poorly.

  • Specific new skills you will gain — not skills you already have. Name the techniques, courses, or methods. "I'll learn CRISPR screening from Dr. [Name]'s lab" is stronger than "I'll develop genetic tools expertise."
  • Named mentorship plan — primary sponsor, thesis committee members, any co-mentors for specific techniques. How often will you meet? How will your committee ensure you develop independence?
  • Career milestones with a timeline — first conference presentation, first paper submission, qualifying exam (if not yet done), thesis defense, postdoc application start date.
  • Career goals beyond the PhD — academia, industry, government, policy. Reviewers want to fund someone with a plan for where they're going.
  • Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) plan — required. Describe the formal training you'll receive. This is not optional.

Sponsor Statement & Letters of Support

  • Sponsor's letter matters more than applicants realize. A generic template-sounding sponsor letter is a red flag. It should feel written for you specifically.
  • The sponsor statement must address: mentoring track record (where are previous trainees now?), their plan to ensure your independence, the lab's resources for this project, and how your dissertation fits within but is not simply a subsection of their R01.
  • Request 3–5 reference letters from committee members, rotation advisors, and collaborators who know your research work specifically. Generic letters from distinguished scientists who barely know you hurt more than help.
  • Reference letters go directly to NIH — you do not see them. Provide letter writers with your aims page and a brief memo on what you hope each will emphasize.
  • Biosketch: Use NIH FORMS-I format. Personal statement should connect your background to this fellowship specifically. Include presentations, posters, and preprints even if no peer-reviewed publications yet.
⚠ Review criteria changed January 25, 2025. PA-25-422 (the current parent FOA, issued June 2025) uses a new 3-criterion structure. If you find older resources describing 5 criteria — Fellowship Applicant, Sponsors, Research Training Plan, Training Potential, Institutional Environment — those are outdated. The criteria below are what reviewers use for any application submitted on or after January 25, 2025.

F31 applications are reviewed by NIH fellowship-specific study sections. Three assigned reviewers score three criteria independently (1–9, where 1 is best) and give an overall impact score, then discuss as a panel. Impact scores are averaged across reviewers and converted to a percentile for ranking. Source: PA-25-422, Section V.

Criterion 1 — Candidate's Goals, Preparedness & Potential

Reviewers assess:

  • →How previous experience prepares the candidate for the proposed plan — considering their stage of training
  • →Whether candidate and sponsor statements and referee letters show scientific understanding, creativity, curiosity, resourcefulness and drive
  • →Candidate's potential to benefit from the plan and transition successfully to the next career stage in biomedical research
Criterion 2 — Research Training Plan

Reviewers assess:

  • →Rigor and feasibility of the research project and how it will develop the candidate as a research scientist
  • →Goals of the overall training plan and whether they will facilitate the attainment of those goals
  • →Whether the plan identifies areas of needed development and contains appropriate, realistic activities and milestones
  • →Whether sponsor(s), scientific environment, facilities and resources are adequate for the plan
Criterion 3 — Commitment to Candidate, Mentoring & Training Environment

Reviewers assess:

  • →Whether the sponsor presents a strong mentoring plan appropriate to the candidate's needs and goals
  • →Whether the sponsor's and organization's commitment is appropriate, sufficient, and aligned with the research training plan
  • →Whether the level of commitment will contribute to successful completion and allow the candidate to advance to a productive career
Overall Impact score: Reviewers provide an impact score reflecting "the likelihood that the fellowship will enhance the candidate's potential for, and commitment to, a productive independent scientific research career in a health-related field." Scores run 1–9 (1 = best); the three reviewers' overall impact scores are averaged and multiplied by 10 (range: 10–90). This converts to a percentile against all applications in that round. Whether you're funded depends on your IC's payline — typically the 10th–25th percentile depending on the institute. Check your target IC's website before submitting. Additional (unscored) considerations include: Human subjects protections, vertebrate animals, biohazards, Responsible Conduct of Research plan, and budget justification.

Specific Aims — 10 commandments

  • Get to the research by sentence 4. The first paragraph is not a history lesson — it's an argument. Gap → problem → your answer.
  • State a testable hypothesis — not a question, not a goal. "We hypothesize that [X] regulates [Y] through [Z]."
  • Limit yourself to 2–3 aims maximum. Three tightly scoped aims is stronger than four sprawling ones.
  • Make aims independent where possible — if Aim 2 requires Aim 1 to succeed perfectly, reviewers will penalize the fragility.
  • End with a compelling "payoff" paragraph — what will the field know? What will change? Why does NIH benefit from funding this now?
  • Write for a general biomedical audience — your reviewer holds a PhD in your field, not your subfield. Define key terms.
  • Every aim should connect back to your central hypothesis — they should feel like a coherent plan, not a wishlist of experiments.
  • Show pilot data or feasibility in the aims page wherever possible — even one sentence referencing a figure in the approach counts.
  • Use your advisor's feedback aggressively on the aims page. It's the one page that determines whether reviewers read everything else carefully.
  • Read it aloud to someone outside your lab. If they can't follow the logic, rewrite.

Research Strategy — section by section

  • Significance: Don't write a literature review. Write a focused argument for why this specific gap must be filled — and tie it to human health outcomes. End with a clear statement of what changes when your work is done.
  • Innovation: Not required to invent a new technique. Using a tool in a new context, combining approaches, or asking a genuinely unexplored question all qualify. Be specific — "this is innovative because [specific reason]," not just "this is the first study of X."
  • Approach: Include preliminary data. A figure showing pilot results can be worth half a page of text. Even a power calculation showing feasibility makes a difference to reviewers skeptical about a trainee's execution ability.
  • For every high-risk step in the Approach, include at least one alternative approach. This is not a sign of weakness — it's a sign of scientific maturity.
  • Include explicit success metrics for each aim. What does "it worked" look like? This is a scored criterion — do not leave reviewers guessing.
  • Don't propose more than you can finish. The F31 is for 2–3 years of work. Overpromising is the #1 Approach critique. Depth over breadth.

Training Plan — don't treat as an afterthought

  • List specific, named skills you will learn — not general competencies you already have. "I will gain expertise in single-cell RNA sequencing through Dr. [Name]'s weekly training" is stronger than "I will develop bioinformatics skills."
  • Include a timeline with concrete milestones: first paper draft, conference presentation, qualifying exam, postdoc search, thesis defense. Reviewers want a scientist with a plan.
  • Describe your mentorship team by name. Primary sponsor, committee members, any co-mentors or external collaborators. How often do you meet? How do they ensure you develop independence from your PI?
  • State your career goal clearly — faculty, industry, government, policy. Reviewers want to fund someone who has thought about where they're going beyond the PhD.
  • RCR plan is required and scored. Name the actual RCR courses, seminars, or workshops at Meharry or your training program. Don't let this be a blank check item.

Resubmission (A1) — don't treat rejection as a dead end

  • Many successful F31 awardees were funded on their A1 (first resubmission). A rejection is the beginning of a revision process, not an end.
  • The A1 includes a 1-page Introduction explaining how you addressed reviewer concerns. This is the first thing reviewers read — it sets the tone. Be direct and specific: "Reviewer 2 noted that Aim 1 lacked power analysis. We have added a power analysis (p. 4) with an increased sample size."
  • Address every concern — even minor ones. Acknowledge the point, explain what changed, point to the page. Skipping minor concerns signals you didn't take review seriously.
  • Add new preliminary data if you have it. Nothing silences feasibility concerns like results.
  • If multiple reviewers misunderstood the same section, the writing was the problem, not the reviewers. Restructure, don't rephrase.
  • Do not argue with reviewers unless they are factually wrong. Even then, be diplomatic: "We appreciate this concern and respectfully clarify that [X]."
Mistake 1

Treating the F31 like a research grant, not a training grant

The F31 funds YOU, not a project. If reviewers can't see how this fellowship advances your career as a scientist — specific skills gained, career trajectory, mentorship quality — the score suffers regardless of how strong the science is.

Mistake 2

Proposing too many experiments

Three years of work crammed into a 6-page Research Strategy signals infeasibility. Reviewers know you're a trainee — they want a realistic plan with depth, not a 5-aim proposal that no one can finish. Scale back and show mastery of your scope.

Mistake 3

No preliminary data

You don't need a published paper, but showing pilot experiments that support your hypothesis and feasibility makes a massive difference. A single figure of pilot data can often address the most common feasibility concern.

Mistake 4

Interdependent aims

If Aim 2 requires Aim 1 to succeed perfectly and Aim 1 is high-risk, your whole proposal looks fragile. Design aims to be as independent as possible. If they must be sequential, include alternatives if the first aim doesn't fully succeed.

Mistake 5

A generic or template sponsor letter

Reviewers read hundreds of sponsor letters. A one-page letter that could have been written for any student tells them your PI isn't invested. The sponsor letter should describe your specific project, your track record in that lab, and your individual development plan.

Mistake 6

Not tailoring the application to your target IC

NIH has 27 Institutes and Centers. A proposal about health disparities in AI diagnostics sent to the wrong IC will confuse reviewers. Know your IC's mission, check their payline, and read their recently funded F31s through NIH Reporter.

NIH Intramural Research

GPP — Graduate Partnerships Program

The GPP lets you conduct your dissertation research in an NIH Intramural Research Program (IRP) laboratory while remaining enrolled at your home university. The stipend is substantially higher than the F31, health insurance is covered, and the research environment is unmatched. Critically, international students are eligible.

Individual Partnership

You're already enrolled in a PhD program and want to create an agreement between your university and an NIH lab.

  • How it worksYou find an NIH PI whose research aligns with your interests, get permission from your graduate program, and register as a GPP student. Your dissertation research is conducted in the NIH lab.
  • DeadlineOpen year-round — no central deadline for individual partnerships. Apply when you've identified a mentor and obtained departmental approval.
  • CitizenshipUS citizens, permanent residents, AND international students on J-1 visas. F-1 visa holders can participate but are limited to 20 hours/week during coursework.
  • Find a mentorBrowse NIH labs at intramural.nih.gov. Contact PIs directly to discuss your interests. No central application before finding a mentor who agrees to host you.
  • Background checkFederal background check (SF-85) required for all GPP participants. Must be approved before accessing NIH facilities.

Institutional Partnership

Your university has a formal partnership with NIH — you enroll in the PhD program specifically designed around NIH research.

  • How it worksUniversities with formal NIH partnerships offer PhD programs where students spend part or all of their doctoral training at NIH. Meharry students should contact SACS about any active institutional partnerships.
  • DeadlineAugust 19 – December 2 annually for institutional partnerships. Check specific partnership for exact dates — varies by institution and IC.
  • CitizenshipMost institutional partnerships require US citizenship or permanent residency. International eligibility varies by specific partnership — confirm with the relevant IC.
  • ApplyApply both to the university's PhD program AND to the NIH GPP Application Center. Some partnerships require both applications simultaneously.
  • Partner schoolsGeorgetown, UMD, Virginia Tech, and 30+ others have formal partnerships. See training.nih.gov/gpp for the current list.

GPP benefits and stipend

Stipend: $51,000/yr (Year 1) — rising annuallyPer the official NIH IRP Predoctoral stipend table (training.nih.gov/stipends, last reviewed July 2026): Yr 1: $51,000 · Yr 2: $55,954 · Yr 3: $57,873 · Yr 4–5: $59,893. Far higher than the F31 ($29,364). All GPP students must be paid at or above the NIH stipend table.
Full health insurance coveredNIH provides a stipend supplement covering the full cost of FAES (Foundation for Advanced Education in the Sciences) health insurance — medical, dental, and vision for individuals AND families.
$1,500 relocation allowanceOne-time benefit for students moving to the vicinity of an NIH campus (Bethesda MD, Research Triangle NC, Cincinnati OH, and others).

📌 For Meharry students: The GPP is an exceptional opportunity for students in SACS whose research in federated learning, AI for healthcare, or data science aligns with NIH IRP labs at NCATS, NIBIB, NCI, or NIMHD. Contacting NIH PIs directly and early is the key first step — there is no centralized application before that conversation.
Other Mechanisms

F30, F99/K00, and Diversity Supplements

Additional NIH pathways depending on your degree type, stage of training, and background. Read these carefully — they are often underutilized by eligible students.

F30 — MD/PhD and Dual-Doctoral Degree Fellowship

For students pursuing combined MD/PhD, DO/PhD, DDS/PhD, DVM/PhD, or similar dual-degree programs. Both the clinical and research training are supported.

Stipend (FY2026)$29,364/year (NRSA predoctoral level)
Tuition cap (FY2026)Up to $21,000/year (60% of actual tuition) — higher than the standard F31 cap of $16K, because F30 covers dual-degree training. Per NOT-OD-26-044.
DurationUp to 6 years total NRSA support
Application timingWithin 48 months of matriculation; no later than 24 months into dissertation research
Research requirementAt least 50% of the award period must be full-time graduate research training
  • NIH will not provide funding beyond your 8th year in the dual-degree program
  • You must have advanced to PhD candidacy by the time of award
  • FOA differs depending on whether your institution has an NIH-funded MSTP (Medical Scientist Training Program) — check which parent FOA applies to your institution
  • If your dual-degree program has no gap between PhD completion and postdoc (no clinical training period between), you may be eligible for the F99/K00 instead — contact the IC program officer to confirm
  • Same citizenship requirements as the F31: US citizens and permanent residents only

F99/K00 — Predoctoral to Postdoctoral Transition Award

Two-phase award bridging your final dissertation years into a fully-funded postdoctoral position. One of the most underutilized awards — many mentors don't know it exists.

F99 phase (predoc)$29,364/yr stipend + tuition + $4,550 institutional allowance; 1–2 years
K00 phase (postdoc)$75,000/yr salary + $25,000 career development support; 3–4 years
Apply whenYears 3–5 of PhD; must need 1–2 more years to finish dissertation at time of award
Next standard deadlineAugust 8, 2026 (see active FOA PA-27-037)
  • You do NOT need to know where you will do your postdoc when you apply — you identify a postdoc mentor during the F99 phase
  • NCI requires the K00 postdoc to be cancer-focused; the F99 dissertation can be in any biomedical area. Other ICs have their own requirements — check each IC
  • NCI applicants are expected to pursue postdoc positions at a different institution from their PhD institution
  • Transition from F99 to K00 is not automatic — you must complete your PhD and activate the K00 phase separately
  • Cannot hold simultaneously with F31 or F30 — you would need to relinquish the earlier award. Plan your application timing accordingly
  • Not all ICs participate — check the current FOA participant list before beginning your application

🌟 Diversity Supplement (PA-23-189) — Often the Fastest Path to Funding

  • Your PI applies, not you. The diversity supplement is an administrative supplement added to an existing, active NIH research award (R01, R21, R03, etc.).
  • No fixed deadline — applications are accepted on a rolling basis, though many ICs have informal windows. Faster to fund than an individual fellowship.
  • Eligibility: Students from underrepresented racial/ethnic groups, individuals with disabilities, individuals from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds, or individuals from rural areas. Check the FOA for current NIH definitions.
  • Stipend: Covers your stipend at NRSA levels plus training-related costs. In many cases pays more than an F31 because it does not have the same tuition cap structure.
  • The student must not already be listed as personnel in the budget section of the parent grant. Ideally you are a new addition to the lab.
  • Research must be within the scope of the parent grant — you cannot propose an entirely different project. Work with your PI to frame your project as a component of their funded research.
  • For Meharry students: If your PI has an active NIH award and you are from an underrepresented group, this may be the fastest route to funded training. Ask your potential advisor whether they have an active award that could support a supplement.
  • Not mutually exclusive with an individual fellowship — you can hold a diversity supplement and later apply for an F31 once eligible (though not simultaneously with another NRSA award).
⚠ F31-Diversity — Current Status Notice (Updated September 2026)
The NIH Ruth L. Kirschstein NRSA Individual Predoctoral Fellowship to Promote Diversity in Health-Related Research (F31-Diversity) was a parallel track to the standard F31, designed specifically for predoctoral students from groups underrepresented in biomedical research. In 2025, NIH terminated a significant number of F31-Diversity awards along with other grants, citing policy changes under new agency leadership. Some of these awards were subsequently reinstated after appeals; others were not. The funding opportunity landscape for diversity-specific mechanisms has been evolving throughout 2025–2026.

Action required: Before beginning a diversity-specific F31 application, verify that (1) the F31-Diversity FOA is currently active at grants.nih.gov, (2) your target IC is participating, and (3) awards in this mechanism are currently being funded. Contact the relevant IC program officer directly for current program status. The standard F31 (PA-25-422) may be the safer path while the landscape stabilizes.

Application Strategy

Writing Guide — Across NIH Fellowships

The principles below apply to F31, F30, and F99/K00 applications. The format and page limits differ by mechanism — the strategic thinking is the same.

The Specific Aims page — universal framework

Shared across F31, F30, F99/K00, and most NIH mechanisms. One page. Gets read first. Often determines the score before anything else is read.

  • Open with the problem, not the history. Sentence 1 should explain the health relevance. Sentence 3 should identify the specific gap. By sentence 5, reviewers should understand exactly why this project is necessary.
  • The central hypothesis must be testable and falsifiable. "We will show that X" is not a hypothesis. "We hypothesize that X regulates Y through Z, because [evidence A and B]" is a hypothesis.
  • Name 2–3 specific aims — numbered, each with one sentence of scientific approach. Don't describe the methods here — describe the scientific logic.
  • The payoff paragraph tells reviewers what the field gains. What will scientists, clinicians, and patients know or be able to do when your work is complete?
  • At Meharry, connect your Specific Aims to health outcomes for underrepresented or underserved populations wherever scientifically appropriate — this directly maps to multiple NIH ICs' missions.

Framing NIH fellowships vs. NSF GRFP

  • NIH fellowships are training grants, not research grants. The most common F31 mistake is writing it like an R01. Reviewers expect you to describe what you will learn and how you will become a productive scientist — not just what you will discover.
  • NIH ties directly to human health impact. Every NSF application emphasizes Broader Impacts broadly (society, STEM diversity). NIH reviewers want to see the pathway from your work to improved health outcomes — even in basic science applications.
  • Preliminary data matters more for NIH than NSF. For the NSF GRFP, a strong conceptual proposal without pilot data can still score well. For the F31, reviewers actively look for evidence you can execute — pilot data, power calculations, and feasibility are weighted heavily.
  • Your mentor is a scored criterion in NIH fellowships but not in the NSF GRFP. Choose your sponsor carefully and invest time in getting them to write a specific, enthusiastic sponsor letter.
  • Apply for both. Many students apply for NSF GRFP before or during year 1, then the F31 in years 2–3. NSF GRFP provides 3 years of support — you can apply for an F31 to use after GRFP ends. Holding both sequentially is a strong CV signal.

Choosing your NIH Institute

  • Your application goes to the IC whose mission best matches your research. For Meharry SACS students: NCATS (translational science, data science), NIMHD (health disparities), NIBIB (biomedical imaging/engineering, AI), NCI (cancer data science), NHLBI (cardiometabolic).
  • Contact the IC's program officer before submitting. They can confirm your application is appropriate and advise on tailoring. This call is often the most valuable 30 minutes you invest.
  • Browse NIH Reporter (reporter.nih.gov) to find recently funded F31s in your area at your target IC — read their abstracts to understand the IC's priorities and what "successfully funded" looks like.

Health equity framing for NIH

  • NIH has a statutory mandate to address health disparities. Research that advances understanding of or interventions for health disparities gets strong reception at NIMHD, NCI, NHLBI, and other ICs.
  • If your dissertation research touches AI in healthcare, federated learning, or privacy-preserving methods for clinical data, explicitly frame how your work supports equitable access, underserved population benefit, or reduction of algorithmic bias.
  • The NIH Minority Health and Health Disparities Strategic Plan (2021–2025) identified five thematic areas — research you can align with these themes strengthens both IM and BI sections across all mechanisms.

The NIH application process — practical steps

  • Register in eRA Commons — you, your PI, and your grants office all need accounts. Set this up months early, not days.
  • Internal deadline: Your grants office (Sponsored Research Office at Meharry) typically requires your completed application 1–2 weeks before the NIH deadline. Ask for their internal deadline at the start of the process.
  • Reference letters are uploaded by letter writers directly in eRA Commons. They receive an automated system notification — verify they have received and responded to it well before the deadline.
  • Application guide: Use the FORMS-I (post-January 2025) SF424 application guide from grants.nih.gov. Instructions differ from FORMS-H — don't use an outdated template.
